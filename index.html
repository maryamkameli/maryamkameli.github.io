I study how rendering choices in VR change what people perceive: whether they keep track of where they are, whether they get sick, and whether an avatar feels like a person in the room with them.

In my TVCG paper, the scene turns into a black-and-white line drawing while you travel through it and fills back in with color when you stop. In a study with 36 people, pointing accuracy matched full-color motion and beat teleporting, and cybersickness was lower than with full-color motion. Now I'm building avatars of myself at several levels of realism, from a 3D Gaussian Splatting capture to a MetaHuman to a cartoon, for a study on whether photorealism changes co-presence and trust.

My bachelor's degree is in electrical engineering, and I still like building whole systems, hardware included. I added a LiDAR and a Jetson Nano to a Pepper robot that went into nine Minnesota nursing homes, and in summer 2026 I designed an HD-EMG recording setup and the trial database for a spinal cord stimulation study at the Hennepin Healthcare Research Institute.

I graduate in May 2029 and I'm looking for internships, co-ops, and student researcher roles for 2027.

Research

IEEE TVCG 2026 · presented at IEEE ISMAR

Sketch-like transitions for moving through VR

Moving through a large VR space usually means gliding or teleporting. Gliding keeps you oriented but makes a lot of people sick. Teleporting is comfortable, but after a few jumps it gets hard to say where you are.

I wanted something in between. While the view moves, the scene becomes a line drawing: surface textures drop out and only the edges that describe the room are left. When you arrive, the color comes back. The idea came from architecture, where designers walk clients through sketch-style VR models so they can understand a space without feeling sick.

In a three-session within-subjects study, 36 participants took a guided tour of a virtual museum with each method. Pointing accuracy with the sketch transition was significantly better than with teleporting and statistically equivalent to full-color travel, and post-exposure cybersickness was significantly lower than with full-color travel.

Hype-XR workshop, IEEE ISMAR 2026 · proposed study

Does photorealism make an avatar feel more present?

Much of what VR research says about avatar realism was measured with avatars that never looked real, and stylized avatars often did as well as realistic ones. Gaussian splatting can now produce avatars that look close to video, so it is worth asking again.

I built three avatars of myself. The captured one is a GaussianAvatars model trained on a 25-camera capture in an ordinary room instead of a studio. The 3DGS rasterizer assumes a centered principal point, so a plain crop around my head rendered nothing. Instead I re-aim each camera at the tracked head with a rotation-only homography and fold lens undistortion into the same resampling step, which made the training images 44% sharper. The crafted avatar is a MetaHuman built from an iPhone scan, and the stylized one comes from Meta's avatar tool.

The planned study puts 36 participants in VR with each avatar and asks about co-presence, trust, eeriness, realism, and how much the avatar resembles the real person. Realistic is not the same as recognizable, so a key question is whether identity fidelity matters more than visual realism when the avatar is someone you know. It also tests whether PSNR, SSIM, or LPIPS predict any of those answers. Longer term, I'm working toward a pipeline that pairs 3D Gaussian Splatting with diffusion models, so one identity can be rendered at any level of realism instead of three fixed points.

Oct 2026 – present · with a research collaborator

A teacher-guided, GRPO-style method for math reasoning in Qwen3-0.6B. I built the training data: 26.8k problems with correct and incorrect solutions, filtered, re-graded, and scored per token. LLM-assisted coding for a qualitative study

Sept 2026 – present · University of Minnesota

Tools for a study of how workers with chronic illness talk about algorithmic management on Reddit. The collector sweeps whole subreddits by date from 2018 on instead of searching keywords, so recall isn't capped by a keyword list. Posts then go through sentence embeddings, zero-shot NLI, and LLM coding (Ollama locally, plus a vLLM batch job for MSI's A100 nodes). The plan is to check the LLM's codes against human coding with Cohen's kappa, ablations, and McNemar's test before relying on them.

Engineering and Health Projects

HD-EMG recording and a clinical-trial database

Jun – Aug 2026 · Principal Research Assistant, NeuroRemap Lab (PI: Prof. Tay Netoff), Hennepin Healthcare Research Institute

The lab studies epidural spinal cord stimulation for people with spinal cord injury. I designed the high-density EMG system that maps stimulation patterns to leg-muscle activation: flexible 128-contact electrode sleeves for the thigh and calf, laid out in KiCad, each read by a 36 × 26 mm headstage built around two bare-die Intan RHD2164 chips, for 512 channels across both legs. The main layout trade-off was spatial sampling against electrode impedance and motion artifacts. I took the arrays through DFM review with the fab house until they were released for fabrication in October 2026. I also built the normalized SQLite database and analysis pipeline for the 12-month trial, which tunes stimulation with Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization on each patient's preferences, so the clinical team can compare patients and cluster subgroups.

Mapping and navigation for a social robot

Jan 2022 – Jan 2023 · University of Minnesota Duluth

Pepper's own sensors weren't enough to map a nursing home: the camera's field of view is narrow, the odometry drifts, and the navigation stack is closed source. We mounted a 360° LiDAR and an NVIDIA Jetson Nano on the robot and offloaded mapping, localization, and path planning to them over ROS (Hector SLAM, AMCL, move_base). The robot was deployed in nine Minnesota nursing homes, with a web app showing the live facility map and a dashboard where staff set up activity sessions like bingo, yoga, and conversation.

Selective classification for medical images

Deep Learning (CSCI 5527) · Spring 2026 · team project

A classifier for medical images should be able to say “not sure” and pass the case to a clinician. I built our experiment pipeline, which scores every prediction with ten confidence measures plus MC Dropout and tracks how the error rate falls as the model abstains on more cases. We ran it on PathMNIST, ISIC 2019 skin lesions, and CheXpert chest X-rays, and stress-tested it with corrupted inputs (noise, blur, brightness, contrast) and with classes held out of training. The best confidence measure was different on each dataset. Weighting each error by its clinical severity raised the risk 2.5 times on ISIC (AURC 0.093 to 0.235) and 3.6 times on CheXpert, so plain error rates understate what the mistakes cost. I also fixed an MC Dropout bug that left batch norm in training mode, which had pulled MC Dropout accuracy on ISIC down to 33% (76% after the fix).

Markerless motion capture

Computer Graphics (CSCI 5607) · Fall 2025 · with Aaron Glick

A semi-automatic pipeline from video to animation. ViTPose gives a 17-keypoint pose for every frame, and our OpenGL viewer draws it as a color-coded skeleton. Pose estimates jitter from frame to frame, so we compared Butterworth and Savitzky-Golay filtering with spline interpolation and approximation. For 3D, SAM 3D Body recovers a body mesh from single images, and we mapped top-view keypoints of a crawling baby onto a rigged 3D model in Unity to animate it.

Segmenting objects behind fences and glass

Course project · Spring 2024 · team of four

When one object is seen through another, like a dog behind a picket fence, SAM tends to cut the background object into pieces along the occluder. We collected 146 images with fences, windows, reflections, and repeating patterns to see where SAM breaks, then tried two fixes. The first merges SAM's masks using Depth Anything: masks whose average depths differ by less than a small threshold become one object. On a photo of a woman holding a glass of water, that cut the segment count from 31 to 17 and joined the water into a single segment. The second used classical template matching, local binary patterns combined with Fourier similarity, to find the occluder's repeating pattern.

Sensors and IoT

Sensors and Internet of Things · Fall 2022

Python labs on a Raspberry Pi with Grove sensors: a potentiometer driving a servo and an LED dimmer over PWM, a light sensor wired to a buzzer that plays SOS, a doorbell button that tweets whether the room is dark, Bluetooth RFCOMM sockets, and a thermostat.
