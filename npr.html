NPR Locomotion: Improving Spatial Awareness and Cybersickness with Sketch-Like Continuous Transitions

Maryam Kameli, Koorosh Vaziri, Victoria Interrante

University of Minnesota

Paper (IEEE Xplore) Study video BibTeX UMN news story

The idea

Virtual environments are usually bigger than the room you are standing in, so VR apps let you travel without walking. The two common options pull in opposite directions. Continuous motion keeps you oriented, but watching the world stream past while your body stays still is a well-known cause of cybersickness. Teleporting avoids that, but every jump cuts the visual thread between where you were and where you are now.

The sketch transition keeps the continuous motion and changes what you see during it. As soon as the viewpoint starts moving, the scene switches to a black-and-white, sketch-like rendering: high-frequency surface textures are removed and only the structural edges remain. The viewpoint moves at the same speed as in full-color travel. When it arrives, the full rendering comes back, so every stop is still a detailed scene to look around in.

Architects already use sketch-style walkthroughs to show clients a space without making them sick. We wanted to know whether the same idea holds up for VR travel in general.

Three ways to travel

Each clip shows one move between stops in the museum. The next stop is marked by a small figure standing in the room.

What participants did at each stop

After every move, participants answered four quick questions:

  1. Point to the previous stop and estimate out loud how far away it is.
  2. Do the same for the stop before that.
  3. And for the one three stops back.
  4. Rate their discomfort from 0 to 10 on the Fast Motion Sickness scale.

Pointing and distance errors show how well someone kept track of the space. The discomfort ratings, together with the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire, track cybersickness.

What we found

Thirty-six participants took the guided museum tour with each of the three methods. Full-color travel gave the best spatial understanding and the most cybersickness. Teleporting caused the least cybersickness and the weakest spatial understanding. The sketch transition fell between the two on both measures.

For architectural walkthroughs and similar uses, where people need to understand a space and also stay in it long enough to explore, that trade-off can be worth making. The paper has the full analysis and statistics.
